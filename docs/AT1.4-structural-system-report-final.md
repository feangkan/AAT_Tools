## 1. The chosen system, and what it commits the drawings to

**Reinforced concrete (RC) carries the podium, the core, and the transfer level. Cross-laminated timber (CLT) and glulam carry every residential floor above.** This is not two systems bolted together arbitrarily — each material is doing the job it's structurally and architecturally best at:

| Element | Podium (Class 6, L00–L01/02) | Core (full height) | Tower (Class 3, above transfer) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Walls** | RC perimeter + core walls | RC shear walls (stairs, lift shafts, risers) | CLT loadbearing party walls between studios (optional) + light-frame internal partitions |
| **Floors** | RC slab on grade / suspended slab | RC landings | CLT floor panels, 5-ply, spanning between glulam beams/party walls |
| **Columns** | RC columns, wide grid (~8–9 m) for retail flexibility | N/A (wall-supported) | Glulam columns, expressed where corridor/lounge allows |
| **Beams** | RC transfer beams carrying tower loads down to podium grid | N/A | Glulam beams on the 6.0 m planning grid (alt. 7.2 m) |
| **Roof** | N/A (occupied above) | RC roof over core / plant screen | CLT roof panels on glulam roof beams; steel plant frame for rooftop services |

**Grid logic:** the 6.0 m structural grid aligns CLT panel widths with the 3.0–3.6 m studio module, so panel joints land on partition lines rather than mid-room. Facade, stair/lift position, and setout all follow from this: the RC core is fixed early (it's the fire-isolated egress path under NCC D1/D2), the tower grid wraps around it, and the transfer beam geometry at the top of the podium is the one drawing that has to reconcile the wider retail grid below with the tighter residential grid above.

## 2. Precedent research (from Week 1, carried forward)

### Precedent 1 — Brock Commons Tallwood House (Vancouver, Canada)

18 storeys, ~53 m, completed 2017 — the tallest mass timber building in the world at the time. Designed by Acton Ostry Architects, it uses exactly this project's hybrid logic: a **concrete core** for lateral stability and fire-isolated egress, **glulam columns** carrying gravity load storey to storey, and **CLT floor panels** spanning between them as both structure and fire-rated diaphragm. Built as student residence for UBC. Once the timber structure started, it was erected by a crew of nine installers in roughly 70 days — hard evidence for the construction-speed claim this hybrid makes.

### Precedent 2 — La Trobe University Student Accommodation (Bundoora, Melbourne)

Two six-level buildings, 624 beds, designed by Jackson Clements Burrows Architects, built by Multiplex — the largest mass timber project in Victoria by volume at completion. Over 75% of gross floor/roof area and over 90% of loadbearing walls/columns are CLT and glulam, with concrete/steel handling transfer and core conditions — the same Hybrid B logic. Built in the same city as this site, using the same Victorian CLT/glulam supply chain (Wodonga CLT, Victorian-manufactured glulam) this report recommends in Section 3. This is the strongest available proof that Hybrid B is buildable, fundable, and approvable in Melbourne specifically — not just in principle.

Together: Brock Commons proves the system works at height; La Trobe proves it works here, at this scale of student accommodation, with this supply chain.

## 3. How the structural materials are made

### 3.1 Concrete — production

Concrete is a manufactured composite, not a raw material: **cement + aggregate (sand and crushed stone) + water**, combined in a batching plant to precise proportions, then transported to site (or precast off-site) before it sets. Portland cement itself is made by heating limestone and clay in a kiln to ~1450°C to produce clinker, which is then ground with gypsum — this kilning step is the single largest source of concrete's embodied carbon (both the fuel burned and the CO₂ chemically released from the limestone itself). Structural reinforcement (deformed steel reinforcing bar, "rebar") is placed in formwork before the wet concrete is poured, giving concrete its tensile capacity — plain concrete is strong in compression but weak in tension. For this project, concrete arrives two ways:

- **In-situ (poured on site):** used for the podium slab, transfer beams/slab, and core walls — formwork is built, rebar cages placed, concrete pumped in, then cured (moist, temperature-controlled) for typically 28 days to reach full design strength, though formwork can usually be struck well before that.
- **Precast (cast off-site, craned into place):** an option for repetitive core wall panels or podium elements — faster on-site programme, more consistent finish quality (relevant to the exposed, "engineered to last" concrete surface this project wants), but requires early design lock-in and heavy crane capacity.

### 3.2 CLT — production

CLT starts as kiln-dried softwood boards (locally, XLam's Wodonga plant uses regionally grown softwood), planed to consistent thickness and graded for strength. Boards are arranged into layers — typically 3, 5, or 7 — with **each layer rotated 90° to the one beneath it**, structural adhesive applied between layers, then the whole stack is pressed (hydraulic or vacuum press) to bond it into a single solid panel. After pressing, panels go through a CNC machining line that cuts them to exact size and pre-cuts window/door openings, service penetrations, and connection pockets specified in the structural drawings — panels arrive on site ready to lift and fix, not to be cut. This prefabrication step is why CLT compresses on-site programme so dramatically compared to poured concrete or masonry.

### 3.3 Glulam — production

Glulam starts the same way (graded, kiln-dried timber), but instead of cross-laminating panels, individual boards are **finger-jointed end-to-end** — a zigzag profile machined into each board end, glued, and pressed — to build up continuous lengths far longer than any single tree could provide. These finger-jointed lengths are then stacked **all facing the same grain direction** (unlike CLT's cross-orientation), glued face-to-face, and clamped or pressed into the final beam or column section — clamping jigs can be straight or curved, which is how glulam achieves cambered beams or curved forms. After curing, the member is planed to final dimension. The result is a linear structural member — a beam or column — with strength concentrated in one direction, which is exactly the complementary role CLT panels don't perform.

## 4. Forms and shapes each material comes in

| Material | Typical forms/shapes available |
|---|---|
| **Concrete** | In-situ poured to any formwork shape (walls, slabs, transfer beams, columns); precast panels (flat, ribbed, or textured-face); post-tensioned slabs for longer podium spans without deepening the structure; off-form finishes ranging from smooth to board-marked/textured — directly relevant to an exposed "raw material" podium finish |
| **CLT** | Flat structural panels, standard widths roughly 2.4–3.0 m (transport-limited), lengths up to ~16–18 m, thicknesses from ~3-ply (~90 mm) up to 7-ply (~280 mm+) depending on span and fire allowance; supplied pre-cut with door/window openings, service chases, and connection pockets machined in |
| **Glulam** | Straight beams and columns in standard rectangular sections; cambered beams (built with slight upward curve to counter deflection); curved/arched members (achievable because lamination thickness can be thin enough to bend before gluing); custom lengths well beyond what a single sawn timber could span, built up from finger-jointed boards |

## 5. Structural connections and details

This is the section that actually makes a hybrid a *hybrid* rather than two buildings stacked on top of each other — every material transition is a connection design decision, not a default.

**CLT-to-CLT (panel-to-panel, floor/wall/roof joins):** half-lap joints, step joints, or surface splines, tightened with self-tapping structural screws driven directly into the panel — no site welding or wet trades needed.

**Glulam-to-CLT (beam-to-column, beam-to-panel):** typically **concealed steel connectors** — a slotted steel plate let into a machined slot in the timber, through which dowels or bolts are driven from the side, hiding all the metalwork inside the timber section. This is deliberately chosen over exposed brackets on this project because it keeps the exposed timber soffit and column faces clean, in line with the Neometro-referenced "structure as finish" aesthetic — the connector disappears, the timber doesn't get value-engineered brackets bolted across its face.

**Glulam-to-RC (the critical hybrid interface — column base at transfer level):** a steel base plate is cast into or post-fixed to the concrete transfer slab with anchor bolts embedded to a specified depth; the glulam column sits on the plate via a shoe or knife-plate connector, with a **moisture break/damp-proof course** between the timber end grain and the concrete — timber end grain sitting directly on damp concrete is a durability failure waiting to happen, so this detail is non-negotiable.

**CLT diaphragm-to-RC core (lateral load path):** steel **collector elements** (straps or angles) tie the timber floor diaphragm into the concrete core, so wind/seismic load picked up across the timber floor plate has a continuous path into the core shear walls rather than relying on friction alone.

**RC transfer beam-to-podium column:** conventional reinforced concrete detailing — continuous reinforcement through the joint, adequate development length — standard RC practice, but the transfer beam's depth and reinforcement have to be sized for point loads landing from the glulam columns above, not just the podium's own retail loading.

**The four connection details this project should draw at 1:5** (already flagged in the design handoff and repeated here because they're the ones a reviewer will look for): (1) glulam column → steel base plate → RC pedestal with moisture break; (2) CLT panel → glulam beam via concealed steel connector; (3) CLT floor diaphragm → steel collector → RC core wall; (4) RC transfer beam/slab at the podium-to-tower interface, including how it achieves the fire separation between Class 6 and Class 3.

## 6. Embodied energy comparison

Numbers vary by study and system boundary, so this section gives ranges from Australian-specific research rather than a single figure, and states what's being compared:

| Material | Embodied carbon | Source basis |
|---|---|---|
| **Concrete** | ~0.36 kg CO₂e per kg of material (low per-kg, but used in very large volumes — slabs, footings, cores, columns — so it's frequently the largest single contributor to a whole building's embodied carbon regardless of its low per-kg figure) | Australian LCA database averages |
| **Reinforcing steel** | ~3.65 kg CO₂e per kg | NABERS default factor |
| **Structural steel** | ~3.9–4.3 kg CO₂e per kg (hot rolled → painted structural sections) | Australian material factors |
| **Mass timber building (whole-building, Australian mid-rise study)** | Mean ~417 kg CO₂e/m² (range 196–590 across uncertainty analysis) | Australian mid-rise mass timber vs. concrete study, Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis |
| **Equivalent post-tensioned concrete building** | Mean ~465 kg CO₂e/m² (range 307–618) | Same study — roughly a 48 kg CO₂e/m² mean reduction for the mass timber building |
| **CLT vs. RC, separate Australian LCA comparison** | ~30% reduction in GHG emissions for CLT-based buildings vs. RC-based buildings of similar scale | Jayalath et al., comparative Australian LCA study |

**Reading this for the brief:** concrete has a genuinely lower carbon figure *per kilogram* than steel, but a hybrid still has to be honest that the RC podium/core/transfer is the carbon-heavier portion of this building relative to the timber tower above it — the sustainability argument for Hybrid B is "substantially lower than an all-concrete building," not "carbon neutral." Framing it that way in the report is more defensible than overselling it. Timber also carries a genuine (if debated in LCA methodology) biogenic carbon storage benefit that concrete and steel don't have at all — the tower itself is holding carbon that would otherwise return to atmosphere, for as long as the building stands.

## 7. How the structural system is applied in a multi-storey building — construction sequence

Hybrid B is built in a specific order, and that order is worth stating because it explains why the programme benefit is real, not just a claim:

1. **Foundations and podium structure first** — piling/footings, then the RC podium columns, walls, and slabs, built using conventional formwork-rebar-pour-cure methods. This portion sits on the critical path and cannot be compressed the way the timber tower above can.
2. **Core walls climb ahead of or with the podium** — often slip-formed or jump-formed so the RC core (stairs, lifts) is always a level or more ahead of the floor construction around it, since it's both the lateral bracing and the only usable vertical access during construction.
3. **Transfer level** — the RC transfer slab/beams are cast at the top of the podium once podium concrete has cured sufficiently to take load; this is the one level where the wider retail grid geometrically resolves into the tighter residential grid above, and it's also where the fire separation between Class 6 and Class 3 is physically formed.
4. **Timber tower erection, storey by storey ("platform construction")** — glulam columns are craned in and fixed to the transfer slab (or to the top of the previous storey's CLT floor), then CLT floor panels are craned in and fixed to the columns/beams below, forming a working platform for the next storey up before the process repeats. Because panels arrive pre-cut with openings and connection pockets already machined, each storey goes up dry, with a small crew, largely without wet trades — this is the phase where the erection-speed precedent set by Brock Commons (roughly 70 days for the entire timber structure) becomes achievable.
5. **Envelope and fit-out follow the structure up**, typically only a few storeys behind the erection front, since the dry timber structure doesn't need to wait for curing the way concrete does.

**Net effect on programme:** the podium is no faster to build than a conventional podium, but the tower above it goes up substantially faster than an equivalent RC or masonry tower — which is the actual, provable basis for saying Hybrid B compresses the overall construction programme, rather than a vague sustainability-adjacent claim.

## 8. First Nations materials, suppliers, and construction methods

This section is explicitly required by the AT1.4 brief and is flagged as carrying forward into the AT3 Project Portfolio — so it's worth treating as a genuine procurement and design question, not a token paragraph.

### 8.1 Certification and procurement pathway

**Supply Nation** is the national body that certifies Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander businesses as genuinely Indigenous-owned — verifying at least 51% Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander ownership, management, and control. For a real project on this site, the practical first step is sourcing through Supply Nation-certified suppliers and contractors wherever the supply chain allows it (fabrication, joinery, and civil/construction trades, not only raw material supply), rather than treating Indigenous procurement as an afterthought once the structural system is already locked in.

### 8.2 Indigenous-owned forestry and timber supply

Indigenous-owned and managed forestry is an active, growing sector rather than a hypothetical:

- The **Tiwi Forestry Project** (Melville Island, NT) is a long-running partnership between the Tiwi Land Council and a commercial forestry operator, generating export timber income that has funded roads, a deep-water port, and community education/health infrastructure for the Tiwi people — an example of Indigenous-led commercial forestry operating at real industrial scale in Australia today.
- **Forest & Wood Products Australia (FWPA)** has published guidance specifically on Indigenous-owned and managed forest lands, identifying opportunities in native forest management, plantation forestry, and community sawmilling, and highlighting the need for further investment in Indigenous commercial forestry — this is a live policy area, not settled infrastructure.
- **Intract Indigenous Contractors**, 51% Indigenous-owned (a partnership arrangement with McMahon Services), is an example of an Indigenous-controlled construction contractor already delivering civil infrastructure work in Australia — evidence that Indigenous-owned firms can be engaged at the construction-delivery end of a project, not only the raw-material end.

### 8.3 What this means for this project, honestly

There is currently **no large-volume Indigenous-owned CLT or glulam manufacturer supplying the Victorian market** at the scale this project needs — the realistic supply chain (XLam Wodonga CLT, ASH glulam) is not Indigenous-owned. The honest position for this report is: (1) prioritise Supply Nation-certified subcontractors and suppliers wherever the project's supply chain allows — civil works, joinery, landscaping, project services — even where the primary CLT/glulam mill isn't Indigenous-owned; and (2) treat species and material selection as a genuine engagement point, not a decoration — a real project on Wurundjeri Country should involve the **Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation** in material and landscape decisions from schematic design onward, consistent with the Acknowledgement of Country already required by the brief, rather than adding it after the structural system is finalised. This is the thread this report carries into **AT3**: procurement strategy and cultural engagement are design decisions, made early, not compliance items added at the end.
