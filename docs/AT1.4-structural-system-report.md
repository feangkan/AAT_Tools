## 0. Reading the design direction first

Before picking a structural system, it's worth being explicit about what "leaning toward 9 Wilson Ave" means structurally, because it should filter every choice below.

9 Wilson Ave is a mixed-use building (apartments, lofts, ground-floor commercial/office) that converts a former industrial backstreet into a pedestrian boulevard. Its architectural language is built on a few repeated moves:

- **Raw, expressed materials** — the building doesn't hide its construction; materiality *is* the architecture.
- **A textured, pigmented concrete facade** ("blush red"), referencing Brunswick's brick-making and rail history — colour and surface come from the primary material, not applied finishes.
- **Robustness that ages well** — described by the developer as "engineered to last," homes that "wear in, not out."
- **Warehouse-to-boulevard conversion** — industrial character retained and refined rather than erased.
- **Ground-floor activation** — commercial/office at street level under residential above, i.e. the same podium/tower relationship as our Class 6/Class 3 split.

**Structural implication:** a system that *hides* its structure behind linings and plasterboard works against this brief. The direction favours a hybrid where **structure is visible and does double duty as finish** — exposed concrete at the podium (mass, texture, "engineered to last"), and exposed timber soffits/columns in the residential floors above (warmth, grain, the domestic counterpoint to the industrial base). This is the lens used in Section 2's recommendation.

## 1. Mass timber typology — every system, with CLT explained in depth

**Mass timber** is the umbrella term for engineered wood products that use large, solid, glued or mechanically-laminated wood sections as *primary structure* — replacing steel or concrete elements — rather than the small dimension studs of light timber framing. All mass timber products work the same basic way structurally: laminating many smaller, graded pieces of wood together cancels out the natural defects (knots, splits) of any single piece, producing a section with predictable, engineered strength at large scale.

### 1.1 Cross-Laminated Timber (CLT) — explained

CLT is the product most people mean when they say "mass timber." It's built from an **odd number of layers (typically 3, 5, or 7)** of kiln-dried timber boards, with **each layer rotated 90° to the one below it** and glued face-to-face under pressure. That cross-orientation is the entire idea:

- **In-plane stability:** because each layer resists the grain movement of the layer above/below it, the finished panel barely swells or shrinks — unlike solid sawn timber, which moves significantly along and across the grain. This is what makes CLT usable as a **flat panel spanning in two directions**, like a thin concrete slab.
- **Panel, not beam:** CLT behaves as a rigid plate. It can be a **floor, a wall, or a roof** — the same product does all three jobs, which is why it dominates repetitive residential typologies.
- **Prefabrication:** panels are CNC-cut off-site (window/door openings, service penetrations, connection pockets all pre-machined), then craned into place and fixed — dramatically compressing on-site programme.
- **Fire behaviour:** CLT chars at a slow, predictable rate (roughly 0.65–0.8 mm/min depending on species). The char layer insulates the timber underneath it, so a sacrificial outer layer can be sized into the panel thickness to deliver a required FRL without needing to fully encapsulate the timber in plasterboard — this is central to how mass timber meets NCC Part B1/Section C.

**Typical role on this project:** primary floor plate across the Class 3 tower, and optionally loadbearing party walls between studios.

### 1.2 The full mass timber family

| System | What it is | Span behaviour | Moisture movement | Typical role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CLT** | Cross-laminated panel, layers at 90° | Two-way (plate) | Stable in-plane | **Floor / wall / roof (primary)** |
| **Glulam** | Laminated layers, all grain **parallel** | One-way (linear member) | Moves like large-section solid timber | **Beams and columns (primary)** |
| **NLT** (Nail-Laminated Timber) | Dimension boards stood on edge, face-nailed together | One-way | High in-plane movement | Secondary / heritage aesthetic — not preferred for multi-storey PBSA |
| **DLT** (Dowel-Laminated Timber) | Boards laminated with hardwood dowels, no glue or metal | One-way | High | Niche — low-tox, fully mechanical fixing |
| **GLT panel** (Glue-Laminated Timber panel) | Edge-glued boards forming a panel, single direction | One-way | High | Secondary panel option |
| **LVL / LSL** (Laminated Veneer / Strand Lumber) | Veneers or strands bonded under heat/pressure | One-way, very consistent | Governed by product grade/thickness | Beams, rim boards, secondary members |

**Why CLT + glulam lead this project:** they're the only two products in the family that are (a) commercially available at scale in Australia, (b) proven at the storey counts a Class 3 student tower needs, and (c) complementary — CLT does the plate work (floors/walls), glulam does the linear work (beams/columns/frame). The others are worth naming in the report to show what was considered and rejected, but they're not structurally competitive for this brief.

### 1.3 Benefits & limitations, system by system

**CLT**
- *Benefits:* two-way spanning reduces beam depth needs; doubles as fire-rated diaphragm and finished ceiling/floor soffit when exposed; fast dry erection; sequesters carbon; excellent acoustic mass compared to light frame when topped.
- *Limitations:* panel weight requires cranage and site access; moisture must be managed during construction (must stay dry before/after install); large flat panels are less efficient than glulam for long clear spans; higher material cost per m² than light timber framing.

**Glulam**
- *Benefits:* long clear spans in one direction; can be cambered; visually expressive (exposed columns/beams read as "structure as architecture" — directly useful for the Neometro-aligned aesthetic); strength predictable and gradeable to AS 1720.1.
- *Limitations:* one-directional strength only (needs a perpendicular system — CLT or steel — to complete the floor plate); larger sections needed as spans grow, competing with ceiling height; connections are the primary design/cost driver.

**NLT / DLT / GLT panels**
- *Benefits:* simple manufacture, low-tech connections (DLT even avoids metal/glue entirely), attractive exposed underside texture, good for cultural/heritage-styled projects.
- *Limitations:* one-way span only, high in-plane movement makes them less dimensionally stable than CLT, weaker as a fire-rated diaphragm, limited local supply chain — not recommended for a multi-storey PBSA tower with tight programme.

**LVL / LSL**
- *Benefits:* very consistent engineered properties, efficient for beams/rim joists and transfer elements, good where section depth is constrained.
- *Limitations:* one-way only, less architecturally expressive than glulam when exposed, typically a secondary rather than primary system in a CLT/glulam scheme.

## 2. Why hybridise, and the three hybrid types (pick one before we finalise)

### 2.1 Why not "all timber"?

A pure mass-timber structure struggles at exactly the points this brief creates problems: **long clear spans at the Class 6 retail podium**, a **structural transfer** where the podium grid changes to the tighter residential grid above, the **lateral (wind/seismic) system**, and the **fire-rated core** that NCC effectively requires to be non-combustible-grade regardless of what's above it (stairs/lifts serving a Class 3 building). Timber alone can technically be engineered to do all of this, but it stops being the economical or buildable choice once transfer structures and tall fire-isolated cores enter the picture. Hence: **hybridise** — put timber where it's structurally and architecturally efficient (repetitive residential floors) and put another material where the brief demands something else (podium spans, transfer, core, lateral stability).

### 2.2 The three hybrid types

---

#### **Hybrid A — Mass Timber + Steel**

Steel beams/columns or a braced steel frame carries the podium and/or acts as the lateral system; CLT panels and glulam frame the residential floors above.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Where steel goes** | Podium retail frame, transfer beams at the podium/tower interface, sometimes braced frame for lateral stability |
| **Where timber goes** | Residential floor plates, columns/beams above transfer level |

**Benefits**
- Steel achieves long clear retail spans with shallow structural depth — useful where Class 6 tenancy flexibility matters.
- Steel + timber can both be prefabricated, so a fully hybrid dry-trade programme is achievable (fast erection on both fronts).
- Steel is lighter than an equivalent concrete transfer structure — smaller footings.
- Slender steel reads as an industrial counterpoint to timber — a legitimate "raw materials" palette.

**Limitations**
- Exposed steel needs fire protection (intumescent paint or board) to hit the FRL a Class 3/Class 6 separation demands — this usually means steel *can't* stay fully exposed the way timber can, undercutting the "expressed structure" idea unless a Performance Solution is engineered.
- Thermal bridging where steel penetrates the building envelope.
- Steel has materially higher embodied carbon than timber or even concrete per equivalent structural capacity — works against the sustainability narrative mass timber is chosen for.
- Differential movement/connection detailing between steel and timber is a genuine design task, not a detail to gloss over.

---

#### **Hybrid B — Mass Timber + Concrete**

A reinforced concrete (RC) podium and/or core carries the Class 6 retail level, houses the fire-isolated stairs/lifts, and provides the lateral (shear wall / core) system; CLT + glulam form the Class 3 tower above.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Where concrete goes** | Podium slab/frame, fire-isolated core (stairs, lifts), transfer level |
| **Where timber goes** | All residential floors above transfer — CLT floors/walls, glulam frame |

**Benefits**
- The RC core NCC effectively wants anyway (stairs/lifts serving a multi-storey Class 3 building need robust fire separation) is now doing double duty as the lateral system — no redundant structure.
- Concrete naturally satisfies the fire separation between Class 6 and Class 3 at the transfer level without extra protective linings.
- Mass and stiffness of a concrete podium/core suit wind loading on a tower typology well.
- **Exposed off-form or pigmented concrete at the podium, paired with exposed timber above, is materially and tonally the closest match to 9 Wilson Ave's raw-concrete + robust-materials language** — the podium can genuinely read as "engineered to last" in the same way the reference project's facade does.
- This is the most built-and-proven hybrid combination for tall timber buildings internationally (see Section 5).

**Limitations**
- Concrete has higher embodied carbon than timber — the sustainability story becomes "minimised, not eliminated," which needs to be stated honestly rather than oversold.
- Concrete needs curing time — the podium/core still sits on the critical path even though the timber tower above erects fast once it starts.
- Transfer detailing (podium column grid vs. tighter residential grid) has to be resolved early and shows up as one of the four connection details expected in AT1.4.
- Heavier structure at the podium increases footing/foundation loads relative to Hybrid A.

---

#### **Hybrid C — Mass Timber + Light Frame (steel or timber stud infill)**

CLT/glulam remains the **primary** structure; light-gauge steel or timber stud framing fills in non-structural partitions, services chases, and sometimes non-loadbearing envelope elements.

| | |
|---|---|
| **Where light frame goes** | Internal room partitions, bathroom pods/wet-wall framing, services voids, sometimes lightweight external screens |
| **Where timber goes** | Everything structural — floors, primary walls, columns, beams |

**Benefits**
- Cheap, fast, and flexible for the fit-out layer — internal walls can be reconfigured without touching primary structure.
- Keeps services (electrical, hydraulics, data) out of the CLT panels entirely, protecting panel integrity and acoustic performance.
- Reduces weight at upper floors compared to using CLT for every partition.
- Very good acoustic separation options between studios when built as a proper stud + resilient channel system.

**Limitations**
- **This is not actually a primary structural hybrid** — it doesn't solve the podium long-span problem or the lateral system, so on its own it can't answer the brief. It has to be paired with Hybrid A or B for anything above a low-rise, and should really be read as a *finishing layer decision* rather than a competing option to A/B.
- Weaker precedent base for tall load-bearing student accommodation compared to A and B.
- Doesn't contribute to the "raw expressed structure" aesthetic — light frame is, by definition, a layer that gets lined and hidden.

### 2.3 Decision table

| Criterion | A · MT + Steel | B · MT + Concrete | C · MT + Light frame |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solves Class 6 long span | Yes | Yes | No (needs pairing) |
| Solves podium→tower transfer | Yes (steel transfer beams) | Yes (RC transfer slab) | N/A |
| Solves lateral system | Braced frame (extra design) | Core does it naturally | N/A |
| Matches "expressed structure" direction | Partial (steel needs fire protection) | **Strong** (exposed concrete + timber) | Weak (frame is hidden) |
| Embodied carbon | Highest | Middle | Depends on primary pairing |
| Built precedent for tall PBSA | Some (RISD, US) | **Strongest** (Brock Commons, La Trobe — see Sec. 5) | Rare as sole system |
| Programme speed | Fast (both prefab) | Fast above transfer, slower at podium (concrete cure) | Fastest for fit-out only |

**Reading of the table against the brief:** Hybrid B (MT + Concrete) is the strongest single answer to the site's actual structural demands (long retail span, transfer, fire-rated core, lateral stability) *and* it's the one that visually tracks closest to the Neometro reference — raw concrete podium, exposed timber tower. Hybrid A remains a legitimate alternative if the studio wants a lighter, more industrial-steel-forward aesthetic instead, and Hybrid C should be treated as a secondary decision (how partitions are built) that sits inside whichever of A or B is chosen, not as a standalone option.

**This is a recommendation, not a decision — tell me A, B, or C (or a variant) and I'll build the rest of the report and the AT1.4 sheets around it.**

## 3. Australian native timber species for mass timber

The brief sits on Wurundjeri Country in the Kulin Nation, and the design direction rewards material honesty — so species selection is worth grounding in what's actually native and regionally available, not just what's commercially convenient.

| Species | Native range | Relevant to this site? | Structural notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Victorian Ash** (*Eucalyptus regnans* / *E. delegatensis*) — trade name for two species; sold as **Tasmanian Oak** when sourced from Tasmania (same/related species, incl. *E. obliqua*) | Alpine Victoria, eastern Vic, SE NSW, Tasmania | **Most regionally relevant** — grown within Victoria | Commercially glulam-manufactured in Victoria today (ASH's MASSLAM range, Heyfield VIC) using Australian/Plantation Oak — genuinely local supply chain for glulam beams/columns |
| **Spotted Gum** (*Corymbia maculata*) | East coast, Vic–NSW border through to Qld | Adjacent, not local | Durable, strong, distinctive figured grain — widely used structurally (rail sleepers, flooring) and popular where exposed timber is a design feature |
| **Blackbutt** (*Eucalyptus pilularis*) | Coastal NSW/Qld | Not local to Vic | Good strength-to-weight, naturally good fire performance among Australian species, economical relative to Spotted Gum |
| **Radiata Pine** (*Pinus radiata*) — plantation softwood, **not a native species** | Introduced/plantation, incl. NE Victoria | Manufactured locally | This is the realistic default for the **CLT panels** themselves: XLam's CLT plant in Wodonga (Victoria/NSW border) is the major domestic supplier. Being upfront that this is a plantation softwood, not a native hardwood, is more honest than implying otherwise. |

**Recommended pairing for this project:** **Victorian Ash / Tasmanian Oak glulam** (regionally grown, commercially available, and it's the species ASH already manufactures as structural glulam in Victoria) for the exposed columns and beams, with **Radiata Pine CLT** from the Wodonga plant for the floor/wall panels — the most realistic, currently-buildable Australian supply chain, while keeping the most visually exposed members (columns/beams — the ones that read as "architecture") in a genuinely native, Victorian-grown hardwood.

**On cultural material significance:** the report shouldn't overstate specific claims here — I don't have sourced information about particular species holding customary significance to the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung people, and it would be inappropriate to assert that without a source. What's appropriate to include in the report is a stated design intent: that species and material selection is an area where **engagement with Traditional Owners / the Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Cultural Heritage Aboriginal Corporation** would be a genuine next step before a real project proceeded, consistent with the site's Acknowledgement of Country obligations already noted in your brief.

## 5. Precedents — hybrid mass timber student accommodation, built

Two buildings, one international and one Australian (and close to home), both hybrid mass timber, both purpose-built student accommodation. Together they're the evidence that this structural direction is not speculative — it's a proven, deliverable typology.

### Precedent 1 — Brock Commons Tallwood House (International)

- **Location:** University of British Columbia, Vancouver, Canada
- **Completed:** 2017 · **Height:** 18 storeys, ~53 m — the tallest mass timber building in the world at the time of completion
- **Architect / structure:** Acton Ostry Architects; structural hybrid system combining a **concrete core**, **CLT floor panels**, and **glulam columns**
- **Structural logic:** the concrete core (stairs, lifts) provides the lateral system and vertical fire-isolated egress — exactly the role proposed for the Footscray core in Section 4. Glulam columns carry gravity load storey to storey; CLT floor panels span between them, doubling as the fire-rated diaphragm and finished soffit.
- **Why it's evidence:** it was erected with a crew of only nine installers in roughly 70 days once the timber structure started — demonstrating the construction-speed benefit hybrid mass timber claims actually holds at real scale, on a genuine student residence, not a demonstration project.

### Precedent 2 — La Trobe University Student Accommodation (Australian, Melbourne)

- **Location:** La Trobe University, Bundoora campus, Melbourne — the same city as the Footscray site
- **Architect:** Jackson Clements Burrows Architects · Builder: Multiplex
- **Scale:** two six-level buildings delivering 624 student accommodation beds, described as the largest mass timber project in Victoria by volume at the time of completion
- **Structural logic:** predominantly **CLT and glulam** — over 75% of the gross floor/roof area and over 90% of loadbearing walls and columns are CLT/glulam, with concrete/steel handling the remaining transfer and service-core conditions, consistent with a Hybrid B approach.
- **Why it's evidence:** it is a **Victorian-built, Victorian-supplied** hybrid mass timber student accommodation project — meaning the exact supply chain (Wodonga CLT, Victorian glulam) proposed in Section 3 has already been used successfully at large scale, for the exact building typology (Class 3-equivalent managed student housing) this project is designing. The design brief also notes the facade palette was developed with reference to the site's Indigenous landscape context — a design move directly relevant to this project's own Wurundjeri Country siting.

**Together, these two precedents cover both ends of the risk question a reviewer will ask:** *"has this been built at this height, and has it been built here, with this supply chain?"* Brock Commons answers the first; La Trobe answers the second.
