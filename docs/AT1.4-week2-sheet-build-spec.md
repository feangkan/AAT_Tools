### Sheet 01 — Chosen structural system: Hybrid B overview

**Narrative:** **Reinforced concrete carries the podium, core, and transfer level. CLT + glulam carry every residential floor above.** This isn't two systems bolted together arbitrarily — three hybrid options were tested against this site's actual constraints (long Class 6 retail spans, a fire-isolated core, a podium-to-tower transfer, wind/seismic stability) before Hybrid B was chosen. Steel (Hybrid A) solves the spans but needs fire protection that undercuts an exposed-structure aesthetic. Light-frame infill (Hybrid C) doesn't solve the podium or lateral problem on its own — it's a partition-layer decision, not a competing structural strategy. Concrete does double duty here: the RC core NCC already wants for fire-isolated stairs/lifts *is* the lateral system, so nothing is redundant.

| Option | Solves long retail span | Solves transfer | Solves lateral system | Matches exposed-structure direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A — MT + Steel | Yes | Yes (steel transfer beams) | Braced frame, extra design | Partial — steel needs fire protection |
| **B — MT + Concrete (chosen)** | **Yes** | **Yes (RC transfer slab)** | **Core does it naturally** | **Strong — exposed concrete + timber** |
| C — MT + Light frame | No (needs pairing with A/B) | N/A | N/A | Weak — frame gets lined and hidden |

**Why this system:** it answers every structural demand the site creates (span, transfer, lateral, fire separation) with no redundant structure, and it's the only option with proven built precedent at this height and typology (see Sheets 02–05).
**Takeaway:** *Hybrid B isn't the "safe middle option" — it's the one where the fire-rated core, the lateral system, and the architectural direction all point the same way.*

### Sheet 06 — How the materials are made: production process

**Narrative:** Understanding how each material is manufactured explains *why* it behaves the way it does structurally. **Concrete** starts with Portland cement — limestone and clay heated to ~1450°C in a kiln, ground with gypsum — mixed with aggregate and water at a batching plant, then either poured in-situ into formwork on site or cast off-site as precast panels; reinforcing steel gives it the tensile strength plain concrete lacks, and it needs roughly 28 days to reach full design strength (though formwork strikes earlier). **CLT** starts as kiln-dried softwood boards, planed and graded, then layered with each layer rotated 90° to the one beneath it, glued, and pressed into a solid panel — that cross-orientation is what makes it dimensionally stable and able to span two ways. **Glulam** takes the same graded timber but finger-joints boards end-to-end into continuous lengths, then laminates them **all facing the same grain direction** and clamps or presses them into a beam or column — a linear member built up far longer than any single tree could provide.

| Material | Core manufacturing step | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete | Kiln (limestone/clay → clinker) → batching → pour/cure | Poured or precast mass, reinforced with steel |
| CLT | Boards layered at 90° → glued → pressed → CNC-cut | Flat two-way spanning panel, pre-cut on arrival |
| Glulam | Boards finger-jointed → laminated one-direction → pressed | Linear beam/column, straight or curved |

**Why this matters:** the manufacturing process is the reason CLT arrives pre-cut and ready to lift (compressing site programme) while concrete has to cure on site (holding the critical path) — the construction sequence on Sheet 12 follows directly from this.
**Takeaway:** *Every programme and cost advantage claimed for this hybrid traces back to one fact: timber is finished in a factory before it reaches site, concrete is finished on site after it arrives.*

**Diagram:** Three-column process strip — concrete (kiln/batching/pour), CLT (layup/press/CNC), glulam (finger-joint/lamination/press).
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §3. APA Engineered Wood Association; XLam Australia (Wodonga plant); naturallywood.com.

### Sheet 07 — Forms and shapes each material comes in

**Narrative:** The shape a material is supplied in directly constrains what the drawings can do. **Concrete** is the most flexible — in-situ pour takes any formwork shape (walls, slabs, transfer beams, irregular cores), precast panels give a more consistent finish (relevant to an exposed podium), and post-tensioning allows a longer podium span without deepening the slab. **CLT** arrives as a flat rectangular panel — typically **2.4–3.0 m wide** (transport-limited, not a manufacturing limit), up to **16–18 m long**, in **3, 5, or 7-ply** builds (roughly 90–280 mm+ thick) — and it's supplied pre-cut, with door/window openings and connection pockets already machined in from the structural drawings, not cut on site. **Glulam** comes as a linear member that can be dead straight, **cambered** (built with a slight upward curve to counter deflection before it happens), or genuinely **curved/arched** — because thin lamination layers can be bent before gluing, which straight sawn timber can't do at any comparable size.

| Material | Standard forms available |
|---|---|
| Concrete | In-situ (any formwork shape) · precast panels · post-tensioned slabs · finish: smooth to textured off-form |
| CLT | Flat panel, ~2.4–3.0 m wide, up to ~16–18 m long, 3/5/7-ply (~90–280 mm+) |
| Glulam | Straight · cambered · curved/arched sections, built up beyond single-tree lengths |

**Why this matters:** CLT's panel-width limit (transport, not manufacturing) is one of the reasons the studio module and structural grid need to be coordinated early — panel joints have to land somewhere sensible, which is exactly the logic behind the 6.0 m grid on Sheet 10.
**Takeaway:** *The material's shipped form isn't a fabrication detail — CLT's transport-width limit and glulam's ability to curve are both directly shaping the plan and the facade before a single connection is even drawn.*

**Diagram:** Sample forms — CLT panel with ply build-up callout, glulam straight/cambered/curved profiles, concrete off-form texture sample.
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §4.

### Sheet 08 — Structural connections: the four details that make it a hybrid

**Narrative:** A hybrid building is defined by its joints, not just by which two materials appear in it — every point where concrete meets timber is a design decision, and getting it wrong is where hybrid buildings actually fail. Four connection types cover every material transition in this scheme, and each solves a specific, named problem: **(1)** the glulam column base has to keep timber end grain off damp concrete permanently, or it rots from the base up; **(2)** the CLT-to-glulam beam junction has to stay visually clean if the soffit is meant to stay exposed, which rules out surface-mounted brackets; **(3)** the timber floor diaphragm has to physically connect to the concrete core, or wind/seismic load has nowhere to go once it reaches the core wall; **(4)** the transfer level has to resolve two different grids *and* form the fire separation in the same pour. None of these are optional extras — each is the answer to a failure mode that shows up in built hybrid projects when it's skipped.

| # | Connection | Problem it solves | Typical solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Glulam column → RC pedestal | Timber end-grain decay on damp concrete | Steel base plate + moisture break/DPC |
| 2 | CLT panel → glulam beam | Keeping the exposed soffit clean | Concealed steel connector let into the timber |
| 3 | CLT diaphragm → RC core | Lateral load needs a path into the core | Steel collector straps/angles |
| 4 | RC transfer beam/slab | Grid change + fire separation, same level | Conventional RC detailing, sized for point loads from above |

**Why this matters:** these are the four details a reviewer will look for first, because they're the actual evidence the hybrid was engineered, not just diagrammed — a section with two materials and no connection detail is a rendering, not a structural proposal.
**Takeaway:** *If only one sheet in this set gets drawn at 1:5 before the Week 2 crit, it should be this one — the connections are where "hybrid" stops being a word and becomes a buildable joint.*

**Diagram:** Four numbered 1:5 connection details, callout to full section.
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §5. Timber Fire Safety in Construction (connection detailing).

### Sheet 09 — Embodied energy comparison

**Narrative:** Embodied energy numbers get quoted a lot in mass timber marketing, so it's worth being precise about what's actually being compared. **Per kilogram**, concrete is genuinely low-carbon (~0.36 kg CO₂e/kg) — the problem is volume: a building uses so much of it (slabs, footings, cores, columns) that it still ends up the dominant contributor in most whole-building assessments, even at that low per-kg figure. Structural steel is far higher per kilogram (~3.9–4.3 kg CO₂e/kg), which is one of the reasons Hybrid A (steel) was set aside on Sheet 01. At the **whole-building** level, an Australian mid-rise uncertainty study found mass timber buildings averaging **~417 kg CO₂e/m²** against **~465 kg CO₂e/m²** for an equivalent post-tensioned concrete building — about a 48 kg CO₂e/m² mean reduction — and a separate Australian CLT-vs-RC comparison found roughly **30% GHG savings**. Fenner Hall's built, audited figure (Sheet 04) — 33% of an all-concrete equivalent — sits right in that range, which is what makes it worth quoting as the "proof," not just the modelled study.

| Material | Embodied carbon | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Concrete | ~0.36 kg CO₂e/kg | Low per-kg, high volume in this hybrid |
| Reinforcing steel | ~3.65 kg CO₂e/kg | NABERS default factor |
| Structural steel | ~3.9–4.3 kg CO₂e/kg | Australian material factors |
| Mass timber building (whole-building) | ~417 kg CO₂e/m² (mean) | Australian mid-rise uncertainty study |
| Equivalent concrete building (whole-building) | ~465 kg CO₂e/m² (mean) | Same study |
| Fenner Hall (built, audited) | ~33% of all-concrete equivalent | Real completed building |

**Why this matters:** the honest claim for this hybrid is "substantially lower than an all-concrete building," not "carbon neutral" — the RC podium/core is still the carbon-heavier part of the structure, and saying so is more defensible under scrutiny than overselling it.
**Takeaway:** *Lead with Fenner Hall's real 33% figure, not just the modelled range — a built number beats a study every time in a crit.*

**Diagram:** Bar chart — kg CO₂e/m² mass timber building vs. concrete building, plus per-kg comparison strip (concrete/steel/timber).
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §6. Certified Energy, Australian material factors; Jayalath et al., CLT vs. RC Australian LCA.

### Sheet 10 — Span report: why this grid, and why it's the efficient choice

**Narrative:** This sheet exists to answer a question directly, with data instead of a round number: **why 6.0 m, and is it actually the cheap/efficient choice or just convenient?** CLT's published span capacity gives the structural ceiling — floor spans are almost always governed by **vibration** (the panel feels springy before it's overstressed), not strength, and a 7-ply panel tops out around 6.1–6.4 m before vibration governs. So 6.0 m sits right at that practical ceiling — it's the widest span achievable without stepping up panel thickness (more timber volume, more cost) or adding an intermediate beam inside the bay. Separately, published cost-per-span studies (glulam girders + CLT floor, cost per m² plotted against grid spacing) show cost rising almost linearly with span in a single-span layout, but an actual **cost-optimum appears near a ~7.3 m square bay with one secondary beam** — smaller grids are consistently cheaper once beam depth (and the timber volume it represents) is accounted for. Both data sets point the same direction: **6.0 m is inside CLT's efficient span range and on the cheap side of the cost curve.**

| CLT panel | Typical floor span (vibration-governed) | Typical roof span (flexure-governed) |
|---|---|---|
| 3-ply | ~3.4–3.7 m | ~3.7–4.3 m |
| 5-ply | ~4.9–5.2 m | ~5.5–6.1 m |
| **7-ply** | **~6.1–6.4 m** | ~7.3–7.6 m |

| Grid study | Finding |
|---|---|
| Single span (no secondary beam) | Cost rises linearly with span — no efficiency gained by going wider |
| Square bay + 1 secondary beam | Cost-optimum near **~7.3 m** — smaller and larger bays both cost more |
| Stepped grid (beam then girder span) | Cheapest at smaller grids, shorter beam spans — minimises beam depth |

**Why this matters:** "6.0 m" isn't an assumption borrowed from a template — it's the number where CLT's structural limit and the published cost-optimum both land close together, which is a genuinely defensible design decision to present.
**Takeaway:** *When asked "why this span," the answer is two independent data sets — CLT's own vibration limit and a published construction-cost study — that happen to agree, not a single justification.*

**Diagram:** Two charts — CLT span-by-ply table (already above) rendered graphically, and cost-per-m² vs. grid-spacing curve with the optimum marked and 6.0 m annotated against it.
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §10. *Structural Basics of Mass Timber* / *Structural Design with Mass Timber* (`resources/5-...` and `resources/6-...`).

### Sheet 11 — Mass timber acoustic detailing

**Narrative:** Mass timber's biggest structural advantage — being light — is also its acoustic weakness, because sound and vibration transmission are **mass-driven**: a lighter floor is inherently more sensitive to airborne and impact sound than an equivalent-depth concrete slab. This matters directly on a Class 3 building, where privacy between unrelated student occupants is both an NCC requirement (Part F7) and a basic liveability expectation. The baseline floor assembly keeps the timber soffit exposed below (nothing hard-fixed to it) while doing the acoustic work above: finish floor, a topping screed, a resilient acoustic mat, then the CLT structural panel. Hitting the **code-minimum** airborne rating (STC 50) needs roughly 100 mm of buildup above the CLT; pushing to a **higher-performance** target (~STC 58, closer to what premium residential briefs ask for) needs closer to 195 mm — **almost double the depth**, which is a genuine floor-to-floor height and cost decision, not a finishes footnote. A second, harder problem is **flanking sound** — noise travelling around a junction rather than through the panel itself — which continuous CLT panels and rigid structural connections make worse, and which research in this area hasn't fully solved yet.

| Target | Buildup above CLT | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Code-minimum (STC 50) | ~100 mm | Standard topping + resilient mat |
| Higher-performance (~STC 58) | ~195 mm | Nearly double the depth — floor-to-floor height cost across every storey |

**Why this matters:** floor buildup depth compounds across every storey of a multi-storey tower, so it has to be locked in during schematic design — deciding it later means either renegotiating floor-to-ceiling heights building-wide or accepting a lower acoustic target than intended.
**Takeaway:** *The acoustic buildup isn't a finishes decision made after the structure is set — at ~195 mm for the higher-performance option, it's effectively adding a partial storey of depth across the whole building if it's not planned for now.*

**Diagram:** Section through typical studio party wall + floor junction, annotated buildup and STC target.
**Footer refs:** AT1.4 Structural System Report §9. *Structural Design with Mass Timber* (`resources/6-...`); WoodWorks Mass Timber Fire & Acoustic Database. ABCB, *NCC 2022*, F7.

### Sheet 12 — Construction sequence: how it's actually built

**Narrative:** The programme benefit claimed for this hybrid is only credible if the build order that produces it is stated explicitly. **(1)** Foundations and the RC podium go first — conventional formwork/rebar/pour/cure, and this stays on the critical path regardless of what happens above it. **(2)** The RC core climbs ahead of or with the podium, typically jump-formed, because it's both the lateral bracing system and the only usable vertical access during construction. **(3)** The transfer slab and beams are cast once the podium concrete has cured enough to take load — this is the single level where the wide retail grid geometrically resolves into the tighter residential grid, and where the Class 6/Class 3 fire separation is physically formed in the same pour. **(4)** The timber tower then erects **storey by storey** — "platform construction" — with pre-cut glulam columns and CLT panels craned in dry and fixed, each completed storey forming the working platform for the next. **(5)** Envelope and fit-out follow only a few storeys behind the erection front, because the dry timber structure doesn't need to wait for curing. The honest summary: the podium is no faster than a conventional podium — the speed gain is entirely in the tower.

| Step | What happens | Speed characteristic |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Podium | RC formwork/rebar/pour/cure | Conventional pace, critical path |
| 2. Core | RC core climbs ahead (jump-form) | Sets pace for everything around it |
| 3. Transfer | RC slab/beams cast once podium cures | Where grid + fire separation resolve |
| 4. Timber tower | Storey-by-storey platform erection, dry | Fast — pre-cut, small crew, no wet trades |
| 5. Envelope/fit-out | Follows a few storeys behind erection | Not waiting on concrete cure times |

**Why this matters:** the specific claim "hybrid mass timber is faster to build" only holds for the tower, not the whole building — stating this precisely (rather than as a blanket claim) is what makes the programme argument hold up under questioning.
**Takeaway:** *The real, provable speed claim is "the podium is normal speed, the tower above is dramatically faster" — Brock Commons' 70-day timber erection (Sheet 02) is the number that backs it up.*

**Diagram:** 5-step construction sequence strip, podium → core → transfer → timber erection → envelope, with relative duration bars.

### Sheet 14 — What is this building under the NCC?

**Narrative:** Classification in the NCC follows **use**, not appearance — which is why this is stated first among the compliance sheets, before the structural response to it. This is a mixed building: **Class 3** student lodging above **Class 6** retail, each classified separately under **A6G1**. Class 3 (**A6G4**) covers long-term or transient accommodation for unrelated persons — a hostel or lodging house, not a Class 2 apartment building, and that distinction is the load-bearing constraint for the entire project: the brief for this course explicitly requires the building never be classified Class 2. The evidence that supports Class 3 rather than Class 2 is programmatic, not architectural — a shared, managed communal kitchen of 120–150 m², studios of 15–25 m² with ensuite (not self-contained kitchens, which would push toward Class 2), and shared laundry, gym, and study facilities. Every subsequent compliance sheet (escape, fire, sanitary, accessibility) follows from getting this classification right first.

| Classification | Basis | Evidence in this design |
|---|---|---|
| Class 3 (A6G4) | Long-term/transient accommodation, unrelated occupants | Shared kitchen 120–150 m², studios 15–25 m² ensuite, shared laundry/gym/study |
| Class 6 | Retail, ground floor | Podium level, separately classified |
| A6G1 | Mixed building, parts classified separately | Class 3 + Class 6 permitted in one building |

**Why this matters:** the Class 2 vs. Class 3 distinction changes almost every downstream requirement (sprinklers, fire separation, escape numbers) — getting it right on this sheet is what makes every later compliance sheet valid.
**Takeaway:** *This building is a managed, shared-facility hostel, not a collection of self-contained apartments — that one sentence is the reason the rest of the compliance strategy looks the way it does.*

**Diagram:** Section — Class 6 podium | Class 3 tower | fire separation line.

### Sheet 15 — Classification consequences

**Narrative:** Once Class 3 is established (Sheet 14), it triggers a specific, non-negotiable set of downstream requirements — this sheet exists to make that chain of cause and effect explicit rather than leaving each requirement to look arbitrary on its own sheet. ### Sheet 17 — Fire resistance & timber (Section C + Part B1)

**Narrative:** The structure has to remain standing for the full duration of the escape strategy on Sheet 16 — this sheet is where the hybrid's fire performance is actually demonstrated, not assumed from the material choice. The strategy layers five measures rather than relying on any single one: compartmentation storey by storey; sprinklers throughout the Class 3 floors; a fire-resistance level (FRL) applied to the structure and to the Class 3/Class 6 separation; and, specifically for the exposed CLT and glulam, an **encapsulation or char-design approach** — sizing a sacrificial outer layer into the panel or beam thickness itself, calculated to char at a known, predictable rate, rather than boxing the timber in plasterboard and losing the exposed finish the whole design direction depends on. The RC podium's role here isn't incidental — reinforced concrete naturally satisfies the fire separation requirement at the retail/transfer level without needing additional protective linings, which is one more reason Hybrid B was chosen on Sheet 01.

| Fire measure | Applied to | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Compartmentation | Every storey | Contains fire spread vertically |
| Sprinklers | Class 3 floors | Active suppression |
| FRL | Structure + Class 3/6 separation | Structural fire performance |
| Char-design / encapsulation | CLT and glulam | Meets FRL while keeping timber visible |
| RC podium | Retail/transfer level | Naturally satisfies separation, no extra lining needed |

**Why this matters:** char-design (rather than full encapsulation) is the specific technical mechanism that lets this project keep the exposed timber aesthetic set out on Sheet 05 while still meeting the NCC — without naming this mechanism explicitly, "exposed timber" and "fire compliant" look like a contradiction.
**Takeaway:** *The exposed CLT soffits and glulam columns shown on Sheets 01–05 are only NCC-compliant because of the char-design calculation on this sheet — this is the sheet that makes the aesthetic legally possible, not just visually appealing.*

**Diagram:** Section — RC podium | timber tower | char layer callout | sprinkler zone.
**Footer refs:** ABCB, *NCC 2022*, Sec. C; Part B1. KLH *Multi-Story Residential*.

### Sheet 18 — F2/sanitary, wet areas, DDA-accessible studio

**Narrative:** Amenity and accessibility requirements are drawn on the same 6.0 m structural grid used throughout this set, so the DDA studio isn't a special-case room bolted onto the plan — it's a standard bay sized to the accessible standard instead of the typical studio standard. Every studio needs an ensuite meeting NCC Tables F2.3/F2.4, alongside communal WC/basin provision; wet areas throughout (ensuites and communal) need waterproofing under Part 10.2. The DDA-accessible studios specifically need to meet AS 1428.1: 30–35 m² (larger than the standard 15–25 m² studio), a 1:20 maximum gradient, clear door openings of at least 850 mm, and turning circles sized for wheelchair access — none of which are achievable by simply enlarging a typical studio's footprint without re-planning the fixture layout around the turning circle first.

|---|---|---|
| Studio size | 15–25 m² | 30–35 m² |
| Ensuite provision | Tables F2.3/F2.4 | Same, plus turning circle clearance |
| Wet-area waterproofing | Part 10.2 | Part 10.2 |
| Accessible gradient | — | 1:20 (AS 1428.1) |
| Clear door width | Standard | ≥850 mm (AS 1428.1) |

**Why this matters:** because the DDA studio is drawn on the same structural grid as every other studio (not a bespoke oversized room), it proves the grid chosen on Sheet 10 is flexible enough to absorb the accessible-unit requirement without breaking the repetitive floor plate the whole hybrid depends on for cost efficiency.
**Takeaway:** *The DDA studio isn't an exception to the grid — it's evidence the 6.0 m grid works for the hardest planning case in the building, not just the easy one.*
